# Read page — fact check of read.json

> **Superseded 2026-10-02.** `read.json` has been rewritten from sources and
> cites them paragraph by paragraph. The check below was done mostly
> against Wikipedia. Going to the sources behind it changed three verdicts:
>
> - **Blue, "worth more than gold": TRUE.** The National Gallery says the best
>   grades "could be worth more than gold". Wikipedia's "as highly as gold"
>   was the weaker source.
> - **Green, "for one century, of arsenic": FALSE, it's longer.** ColourLex
>   has emerald green in use into the second half of the 20th century, so the
>   arsenic greens ran for well over a century and a half.
> - **Orange, 1502: UNCONFIRMED.** Wordorigins finds no document behind the
>   Margaret Tudor date. The earliest documented uses are a 1512 will
>   ("orenge colour") and a 1557 cloth law.

Checked 2026-10-01 against the sources linked below (mostly Wikipedia articles
and Etymonline, so they're secondary sources; each one names the primary source
it cites). Verdicts: **TRUE**, **PARTLY** (true with an error or overstatement),
**FALSE**, **EMPTY** (opinion or filler, nothing to check).

**Bottom line:** none of the body text can be marked verified as written. Every
entry has at least one false claim, and most of the rest is filler. The
**standfirsts** (the italic one-liners) mostly hold up; two need softening.

One problem runs through every entry: **"the monochromatic scale" isn't a term in
colour theory.** "Monochromatic" means tints and shades of a single hue, and
there's no scale that colours get "added to" or "categorised" on. Every sentence
built on it ("orange was officially added to the monochromatic scale in the 18th
century", "purple was added in the 19th century") has no real meaning, so it
can't be true.

## Orange

| Claim | Verdict | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Standfirst: no English word until the fruit lent its own | TRUE | First recorded as a colour name in 1502, in clothing bought for Margaret Tudor; Old English said *ġeolurēad* (yellow-red). | "Not until the 18th century officially added to the monochromatic scale" | FALSE | There's no such scale. Orange's place in Newton's spectrum came from his prism work in the 1660s–70s. [ROYGBIV] |

| Newton called orange a mix of red and yellow, the product of two primaries | FALSE | The reverse: *Opticks* separates pure orange light from red+yellow mixtures, which a prism can split apart. Newton didn't use painter's primaries. [Orange colour] |
| Split into reddish-orange and yellowish-orange, one warmer, one muted | EMPTY | No source; reads as invented. |
| Popular in the 19th century through Impressionism and Expressionism | PARTLY | Impressionism yes (chrome orange, invented 1809; Renoir used it straight from the tube). Expressionism started in the 1900s, which is the 20th century. [Orange colour] |
| Branding, warmth in the home | EMPTY | |

## Yellow

| Claim | Verdict | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Standfirst: "for most of its history, poisonous" | PARTLY, overstated | Orpiment (arsenic), chrome yellow (lead chromate), Naples yellow and gamboge are all toxic. | Pigment use as early as 30,000 BC | UNSUPPORTED | The source dates the Lascaux yellow horse to about 17,000 years ago. Older yellow ochre use is likely, but no source here gives 30,000 BC. [Yellow] |

Chevreul's colour book, on simultaneous contrast, came in 1839. [Primary], [RYB], [Chevreul] |
| On the traditional wheel yellow sits between green and orange | TRUE | |
| …and is the complement of blue | FALSE | On the traditional red-yellow-blue wheel, yellow's complement is **purple**; blue's is orange. Yellow–blue is the pairing for light (RGB), not for paint. [Complementary] |

## Purple

| Claim | Verdict | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Standfirst: thousands of snails per gram | TRUE | 12,000 murex snails gave 1.4 g of dye (Friedländer). [Tyrian] |
| Standfirst: until an accident in 1856 | TRUE | Perkin, aged 18, made mauveine by accident while trying to synthesise quinine. [Mauveine] |
| Egyptians first made purple dye from sea snails | FALSE | Minoan Crete (20th–18th century BC) is the earliest evidence, then the Phoenicians of Tyre. Egypt used the dye but didn't pioneer it. [Tyrian] |

## What this means for read.json

- Keep every entry `unverified`. The banner is doing its job.
- The standfirsts can stay as written, apart from yellow ("most of its history")
  and blue ("more than gold").
- The body text needs rewriting from sources rather than correcting line by line.
  Once the false sentences and filler are removed, about one true sentence per
  entry is left. The TRUE rows above are the material to build from, and each
  comes with its source.
